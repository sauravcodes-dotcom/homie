HOMIE · Rating engine proposal

How should HOMIE score an apartment?

Today HOMIE takes a plain average of the stars. That's easy to read but easy to fool. This page walks through a better approach one idea at a time. You can play with each idea, then run a simulation that compares the methods on made-up USC housing markets.

The problem

Five ways a plain average misleads students

1 review ≠ 100 reviews

A building with one 5★ review beats a building with 120 reviews averaging 4.6. One happy friend of the landlord shouldn't win.

Only the loud review

People who loved or hated a place write reviews. People who thought it was "fine" mostly don't. Research calls this the J-shaped pattern.

Fakes and grudges

Six angry or fake reviews posted in one week can sink a good building. A small push of fake 5★ reviews can lift a bad one.

Buildings change

New management can fix (or ruin) a building. A 2022 review about broken AC says little about 2026.

Everyone cares about different things

One student cares most about safety, another about price and noise. A single number hides that.

Inflation

On Airbnb about 95% of listings sit at 4.5★ or higher. When everything is "4.7", the number stops telling you anything.

The core idea in one line

Start at the campus average. Let trustworthy, recent reviews move you.

Every building starts at the USC-wide average. Each review pulls the score toward what it says. How hard it pulls depends on who wrote it and how recent it is. With few reviews the score stays near average. With many it follows the reviews.

HOMIE score ≈ ( k × campus average + Σ weight × stars ) ÷ ( k + Σ weight )
weight = trust (who wrote it) × freshness (how old it is)
k ≈ "how many reviews it takes to outweigh the campus average"

That's the simple version. The real engine does the same thing with probabilities (a Bayesian model), so it can also say how sure it is and spot reviews that look fake. The demos below use the real engine.

What's going on inside

Six ideas, explained simply

1Borrow strength from campus

No reviews? You get the campus average, labelled "not enough reviews yet". Each review moves the score a bit. Statisticians call this shrinkage. It's the main reason a single 5★ can't put a building at #1.

2Show the range, not just the number

"4.1★ (likely 3.6–4.5)" is more honest than "4.1★". The range shrinks as reviews come in. Sorting the "best buildings" list by the bottom of that range means a building has to prove itself to reach the top.

3Not every review counts the same

Verified residents get the most trust, anonymous accounts the least. The engine also assumes any review might be junk, which caps how far one review can move the score. A burst of untrusted reviews in the same week gets trusted even less.

4Recent reviews matter more

A review loses half its weight every 2 years. When new management turns a building around, the score follows in months, not years. If recent reviews clearly differ from older ones, the page shows "Improving" or "Declining".

5Read the words, not just the stars

HOMIE's AI already turns each review into topics (pests, safety, noise…) marked positive, mixed or negative. Each topic gets its own score, which starts at the building's overall score, so one comment can't swing it much. Serious problems like safety incidents are never averaged away. They're always shown as a count.

6Your score, your priorities

Tell HOMIE what matters to you and it re-weights the topic scores into a personal score. Same reviews, different answer for different students. Try it below.

Roommate ratings

Who should rate the roommates?

Right now the student subleasing the room rates their own roommates. That student wants the room taken, so they have every reason to say "great roommates!" Research on Airbnb found the same thing with hosts and guests: people go easy when the other side might see the review or hit back.

Subleaser's own rating

Treated as a hint, not a verdict. The engine assumes it runs high and is noisy.

5★ from the subleaser alone →

–

+ New tenant, after 30 days

The person who actually moved in rates the roommates after a month. Full trust.

…plus one 3★ from the new tenant →

–

Reveal both at once

Neither rating shows until both are in, or 14 days pass. Airbnb's experiment found this reduced retaliation and brought out more honest negative feedback.

Longer term: how good a roommate is depends on the pair, not just the person. A night owl is a fine roommate for another night owl. So for roommates, "how well will you get along" (HOMIE's lifestyle matching) matters more than a single star number.

Simulation lab

Does it actually work? Test it on fake USC markets.

Each run builds a made-up market of 60 buildings, each with a hidden true quality that only the simulator knows. It then writes 4 years of reviews with real-world problems built in: happy and angry people review more often, raters differ in how harsh they are, some buildings change management, and some get fake review campaigns. Each method sees only the reviews. We check which one gets closest to the hidden truth.

The simulator deliberately uses slightly different settings from the engine, so the engine doesn't get a free pass from knowing exactly how the reviews were made.

What the research says

Where each idea comes from

Miller: Ranking items with star ratings

Don't rank by the average. Rank by a cautious lower estimate, so items with few ratings don't jump to the top. → Ideas 1 & 2

Hu, Pavlou & Zhang (MIS Quarterly 2017): Self-selection bias

Reviews are J-shaped because people with strong feelings post. When everyone was asked to review, ratings looked normal. The average alone is a biased signal. → Why we model raters, and why we should ask every tenant to review

Zervas, Proserpio & Byers: Airbnb, where every stay is above average

About 95% of Airbnb listings average 4.5★ or higher. → Show a percentile ("better than 70% of USC buildings") next to stars

Fradkin, Grewal & Holtz (Marketing Science 2021): Reciprocity on Airbnb

Hiding reviews until both sides submit reduced retaliation and brought out more negative text. → Roommate rating design

Filippas & Horton: Reputation inflation

Ratings creep upward over time on online marketplaces. → Compare each building to the campus, not to a fixed number

Casabianca & Beiting-Parrish (2026): Correcting for rater effects

Some raters are harsh and some are generous. Psychometric models (item response theory) separate the rater from the thing being rated. → Raters are part of the model

de Kerchove & Van Dooren: Iterative filtering

Reputation systems should limit how much any single rater can move a score, which makes them resistant to spammers. → Idea 3 (trust caps)

Cabral & Hortaçsu: Dynamics of seller reputation (eBay)

Reputation changes over time, and recent feedback predicts what comes next. → Idea 4 (freshness)

Prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., Science 2023; 2026 review)

Let AI label lots of data, then use a small sample of human-checked labels to correct its mistakes, with honest error bars. → Idea 5: check a sample of AI topic extractions by hand each month

Aggregate-then-Calibrate (ICLR 2026)

Combining human judgments with model scores beats either one alone. → Stars + AI-read text together

Gill & Swartz: Bayesian analysis of dyadic data

In student residence halls, how much roommates like each other depends on the person, the partner and the specific pair. → Roommate quality is about fit

Two-stage Bayesian ordinal model for ride-hailing reviews (2026)

Uses a Bayesian model built for star ratings and recommends tiered, uncertainty-aware rankings over one fixed list. → Ideas 1 & 2

Proposal

How we'd roll it out

1
Ranges and confidence first. Show "4.1★ (3.6–4.5) · 12 reviews · medium confidence" and sort "best" lists by the cautious score. Biggest win, smallest change.
2
Trust and freshness. Weight verified residents above everyone else, halve a review's weight every 2 years, and flag bursts of untrusted reviews for a moderator.
3
Topic scores and personal scores. Use the AI topic extraction we already have. Always surface serious safety reports as a count.
4
Fix roommate ratings. The new tenant rates after 30 days, both ratings are revealed together, and the subleaser's rating counts as a hint only.
5
Tune on real data. Once there are a few hundred reviews, refit the engine's settings (how harsh students are, how fast reviews go stale) from HOMIE's own data every month, and hand-check a sample of the AI's topic labels.

Everything runs in a few milliseconds per building with no extra infrastructure. Same reviews in, same score out, which keeps HOMIE's rule that "numbers are computed, words are generated".
